Which New Zealand jobs are most exposed to AI

By Ray Yin · October 2026

Each box is a job. The bigger the box, the more people do it. The colour shows how much of the everyday work today's AI tools could do or speed up, from 0 (almost none) to 10 (almost all).

    How this was made

    Where the numbers come from

    Every job, headcount, pay figure and task list comes from Tahatū, the Tertiary Education Commission's careers website. Headcounts and the change since 2018 come from the 2023 and 2018 Censuses. Pay is before tax, and yearly pay assumes full-time hours.

    Tahatū lists 806 jobs. 763 of them have a census headcount and appear on the map. The rest are in the table. 0 jobs have no pay figure.

    Some pay figures cover a wider group of similar jobs, so 53 jobs share exactly the same pay numbers with at least one other job.

    The AI exposure score

    Each job has a score from 0 to 10 for how much of its usual tasks today's AI tools could do, or speed up a lot. Claude, an AI model from Anthropic, read the official description and task list for each job and scored it against this scale in October 2026.

      Jobs were scored in batches by industry, so similar jobs sit side by side, with 21 hand-checked jobs as fixed reference points. Each industry was then reviewed from lowest to highest score for consistency.

      As a check, the scores were compared with Karpathy's US scores for 124 jobs with closely matching titles. His run about 1.5 points higher on average, partly because his count self-driving vehicles and these don't. Where a job differed by 3 or more, its task list was read again, and 15 scores were changed.

      What the score doesn't tell you

      A high score doesn't mean the job will disappear. When work gets faster and cheaper, people sometimes want more of it, so some high-scoring jobs may grow.

      The score is about AI tools that work on a screen. Robots and self-driving vehicles aren't counted.

      It's one model's estimate from a task list, so treat a single point either way as noise. The pattern across jobs is more reliable than any one number.

      The idea comes from Andrej Karpathy's US Job Market Visualizer, built on US government data. This version uses New Zealand data and its own scoring.
